Why a Denver water heater fills a tub slower, and what to check first.

At altitude, a gas water heater makes hot water more slowly than its label suggests. Gas heater manuals call for cutting the input rating 4% for every 1,000 feet above sea level once you pass 2,000 feet. In Denver that is about 21% less heat going into the water, which matters when a walk-in tub needs 35 to 40 gallons of hot water in one go.

Most walk-in tub advice is written for sea level, and so is most water heater advice. Along the Front Range and in the foothills the tank holds the same number of gallons, but the burner behind it is working with thinner air. This guide explains what the derate means for a walk-in tub bath, which heaters are affected and which are not, and what to check before install day. It complements our winter installation guide, which covers colder incoming water and getting the house ready.

What does "derating for altitude" mean for a water heater?

A gas burner needs oxygen. Higher up, each cubic foot of air carries less of it, so a burner set up for sea level can burn poorly unless its gas input is reduced. Manufacturers handle this with a simple rule printed in their installation manuals: ratings apply up to 2,000 feet, and above that the input is reduced by 4% for each 1,000 feet of elevation. The same rule appears in furnace and boiler manuals, and it traces back to the National Fuel Gas Code (ANSI Z223.1 / NFPA 54) that those manuals reference.

Lower input means fewer BTUs per hour reaching the water. The storage tank does not shrink, but recovery, the rate at which the heater reheats a tank after you draw it down, drops roughly in step with the input. Some heaters are built or certified for high-altitude use and carry their own ratings. The rating plate and the manual are the final word for your specific unit.

Here is what that looks like for a common 40,000 BTU per hour tank heater. These are worked numbers from the rule above, not measurements of any one model.

Does the derate actually matter for a walk-in tub bath?

For a single bath, usually less than people fear. For the second hot-water use of the evening, more than people expect. That is the honest summary.

A walk-in tub fill comes mostly out of what is already stored in the tank. The derate does not touch that stored water. Where altitude bites is the reheat: if someone showers at 6:30 and the tub fills at 7:00, a Denver heater has had less time-equivalent of heating than the same model in Houston. Couples who both bathe in the evening, and households where laundry or the dishwasher runs right before bath time, feel it first.

The mixing math shows why the tank size matters so much. To fill a tub to 102°F with 120°F hot water, the share of hot water you need depends on how cold the incoming water is:

They leave out heat lost to a cold acrylic shell and to the pipes, so real-world needs run a little higher in January. A tank heater does not deliver its full rated volume at full temperature either; as cold water enters the bottom, the outlet temperature eventually starts to drop. Which water heaters lose output at altitude, and which do not?

Only fuel-burning heaters are derated, but every type has its own Front Range quirk.

Water heater types at Colorado elevations
Heater typeAffected by altitude?What it means for a walk-in tub
Gas tank (natural gas or propane)Yes, unless listed for high altitudeStored gallons are the same; recovery is slower. Size the tank to cover the whole fill.
Gas tanklessYes, unless listed for high altitudeFlow at a given temperature rise drops. Colder winter water makes it worse, so fill can slow down or run cooler.
Electric resistance tankNo combustion, so no derateRecovery is slow to begin with. The tank needs to hold the full fill.
Heat pump water heaterNot derated like gasOutput depends on the air around it. A cold basement in January slows it; most have an electric backup mode.

Propane is common in the foothills and on the plains east of the metro. The derate applies to propane burners too, and propane heaters at mountain elevations are more likely to have been set up for altitude by the installer. If you are not sure how yours was set up, a plumber can read the orifice and the manual in a few minutes.

How do you check your own heater before ordering a tub?

  1. Photograph the rating plate. It lists capacity in gallons, input in BTU per hour, the first-hour rating on newer units, and sometimes an altitude note.
  2. Find the tub's fill volume. Your installer has it on the spec sheet. Compact soakers and large bariatric models differ by a lot.
  3. Look at the age. The serial number usually encodes the year. An old tank with sediment holds less usable hot water than its label says.
  4. Note what runs before bath time. Dishwasher, laundry and a second shower all draw from the same tank.
  5. Ask about the inline heater. It keeps tub water warm once it is in, which helps on cold nights, but it does not heat the fill.

Front Range cities generally treat a water heater replacement as permitted work (Loveland's issued-permit log lists them, for example), so if an upgrade makes sense it is a job for a licensed plumber with a permit. What are the fixes if your heater falls short?

There is no single right answer, and the cheapest fix is often a habit rather than a heater.

  • Space out hot water use. Give a gas tank time to recover after showers or laundry. At Denver elevations, an extra half hour helps more than it would at sea level.
  • Pick a tub with a smaller fill. A compact or mid-size tub that fits the bather well uses fewer gallons than a deep soaker, and the bath stays warmer.
  • Choose a larger tank or a high-recovery model rated for your elevation, or add a second tank for the bathroom if layout allows.
  • Size tankless for winter at your elevation, not the sea-level brochure figure.
  • Keep the thermostat at 120°F. The CPSC recommends that setting because hotter water raises scald risk, and older skin burns faster. Raising the setpoint to stretch the tank trades a comfort problem for a safety one.

Above 7,000 feet the numbers in the table get larger, and other things start to matter: well water that arrives colder, longer pipe runs in older cabins, and propane delivery schedules in winter. Questions about hot water at altitude

Does altitude affect a gas water heater in Denver?

Yes. Gas water heater manuals call for reducing the input rating by 4% for each 1,000 feet above sea level once you are above 2,000 feet. At Denver's 5,280 feet that works out to about 21% less input, so the tank recovers more slowly between uses. A model listed for high-altitude use may be rated differently, so check the rating plate.

Does altitude reduce how much hot water my tank holds?

No. A 50-gallon tank still holds 50 gallons. Altitude slows how fast a gas heater reheats, so the first bath of the evening is usually fine and the second hot-water use soon after is the one that comes up short.

Are electric water heaters affected by altitude?

Electric resistance heaters do not burn fuel, so they are not derated for altitude the way gas burners are. They tend to recover more slowly than gas heaters to begin with, so size the tank to cover the whole tub fill.

How much hot water does a walk-in tub fill need?

It depends on the tub's fill volume and how cold your incoming water is. As an example, filling 50 gallons to 102 degrees with 120 degree hot water and 45 degree cold water takes about 38 gallons of hot water. Your installer works this out from the tub's spec sheet.

Should I turn up the water heater to get a hotter bath?

No. The Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends 120 degrees Fahrenheit to reduce scald risk, and older skin burns faster. Fix a short supply with a larger or faster-recovering heater, a smaller-fill tub, or by spacing out other hot water use.

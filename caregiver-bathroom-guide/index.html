A Denver Caregiver's Guide to Safer Bathing

If you are helping a parent or spouse bathe, or worrying about them bathing alone, you are not alone. This guide is for the person doing the helping: how to bring it up, what to look at, how to decide, and where Denver-area caregivers can get support.

Starting the conversation

Bathing is private, and for many older adults, needing help with it feels like a loss of independence. The conversation usually goes better when it is about keeping independence rather than losing it.

  • Lead with what they want. “I want you to be able to keep taking baths on your own” lands better than “I'm worried you'll fall.”
  • Use a specific, recent moment. A near-miss, a bruise, a comment that baths are “too much trouble now.”
  • Offer choices, not a verdict. Show them a walk-in tub, a walk-in shower and a roll-in shower and ask what appeals.
  • Bring in a neutral voice. A doctor or occupational therapist recommending a change often carries more weight than a family member.
  • Give it time. It is fine to have the conversation more than once.

Watch a bath routine, respectfully

With permission, notice how your loved one gets ready for and into the bath or shower. You are not judging; you are looking for the moments that are hardest.

Choosing for the person, and for you

When you help with bathing, the setup has to work for two people. A few rules of thumb from installations we have done for families:

  • If you help with every bath, a roll-in or walk-in shower usually gives you more room to stand beside the bather. A walk-in tub has you working from outside the tub wall.
  • If your loved one bathes on their own and you just want them safe, a walk-in tub with a seat and grab bars lets them soak independently with you nearby.
  • If they use a wheelchair or will soon, plan for a roll-in shower and a rolling shower chair.
  • Protect your own back. A handheld shower on a slide bar, a seat at the right height and a door that opens outward all reduce the bending and lifting a helper does.

Bath-time habits that help

  1. Set everything out first. Towel, clothes, soap, shampoo, all within reach of the seat.
  2. Warm the room. A cold bathroom makes people rush. In a Colorado winter, warm the room before the bath starts.
  3. Check water temperature with your hand or a thermometer before the bather gets in, especially if they have reduced sensation in their feet.
  4. Agree on how to call for help. A bell, a voice call or a wearable alert button if they bathe alone.
  5. Take your time. Rushing is when slips happen.
  6. Dry the floor afterward before anyone walks on it.

Look after yourself, too

Helping with bathing is physical work. Bending over a tub wall, steadying someone who slips, and lifting a leg over a threshold strain a helper's back and shoulders. If you are feeling it, that is a sign the setup needs to change, not that you need to try harder. A seat at the right height, a handheld shower, and a fixture with a lower or no threshold reduce the strain on both of you. Ask the person's doctor whether home health or a bath aide visit is available, and accept help from other family members.

When it is a long-distance situation

Many Denver-area adult children have parents elsewhere, and many out-of-state families have a parent here. If you are coordinating from a distance:

  • Ask a local neighbor or friend to be present on installation day.
  • Get measurements and photos from someone on site before making decisions.
  • Ask the installer to explain the walk-through on speaker phone so you hear it too.
  • Call the local Area Agency on Aging for that county; they can tell you about meals, transportation and in-home help in addition to bathing.

Caregiver questions

My parent refuses to consider a walk-in tub. What now?

Start smaller with grab bars, a shower seat and a handheld shower, and revisit later. Sometimes a doctor's or therapist's recommendation changes the conversation.

Can I be on the phone during the consultation?

Yes. Many families join by phone, and we are glad to talk with you and your loved one together.

Is a walk-in tub a good idea for someone with dementia?

It depends on the person. Waiting for a tub to fill and drain can be confusing or upsetting for some people with dementia, and a shower may be simpler. Ask their care team, and consider what routine they are used to.
